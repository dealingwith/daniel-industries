> ...each time we part the bars of conviction and opinion to step out from this cell of certitude, or are evicted from it by some existential cataclysm, we discover that the most interesting parts of life, the most fulfilling experiences, the richest relationships, are found in the spaces between categories, in the unclassifiable, the unnameable, the deliriously disorienting.

[The Marginalian: The Power of Being a Threshold Person: Liminality, Communitas, and the Anti-structures of Possibility](https://www.themarginalian.org/2026/09/04/turner-liminality-communitas/)

I've worked in organizations where priorities seem to shift with the mood of the person in charge. Sometimes there's a new strategic initiative, sometimes an urgent problem that apparently wasn't urgent yesterday, sometimes a sudden interest in the details of a project that had been progressing just fine without executive attention.

I've started thinking of this as *vibes-based management*.

This isn't simply management by intuition, or even management by emotion. Emotions are an essential part of how humans make decisions, and intuition informed by experience can be an extraordinarily useful tool. The problem arises when a leader's emotional state becomes a substitute for organizational knowledge, coherent strategy, and deliberate decision-making.

Put another way, when the boss is anxious, everyone is anxious. And, more importantly, everyone has to *do something* about it.

## The illusion of control

Running a business is an inherently uncertain undertaking. Even very successful companies operate with incomplete information, unpredictable markets, and complex human systems. There is no amount of planning or analysis that will eliminate this uncertainty.

This is an uncomfortable reality, particularly for people accustomed to being rewarded for having answers. Running a business requires not only expertise but emotional fortitude: the ability to tolerate anxiety, ambiguity, and the possibility of failure. When faced with these uncertainties, we're often more inclined to defend ourselves against the discomfort than to accept it. One such defense is control.

When leaders lack sufficient understanding of the work happening within their organizations, they often compensate by becoming more involved in the details. They request more frequent updates, change priorities, insert themselves into decisions, and introduce new processes intended to make the work more predictable. The result is an unfortunate feedback loop:

**The less a leader understands the organization, the more they intervene; the more they intervene, the less opportunity the organization has to develop independent judgment, increasing the leader's uncertainty.**

## Vibes, frameworks, and accountability

There's an interesting relationship between vibes-based management and its seemingly opposite approach: managing through rigid processes, metrics, and frameworks.

I've written before about my suspicion that [Gantt charts, when used as accountability mechanisms rather than planning tools, can be a lagging indicator of organizational dysfunction](/2022/09/13/henry-gantt/). The same suspicion extends to a lot of management frameworks, particularly when their implementation becomes more important than the outcomes they're supposed to produce.

At first glance, this seems quite different from the executive who changes priorities based on whatever is troubling them this week. One organization appears arbitrary and emotional; the other systematic and rational.

But I wonder whether **vibes-based management and process-based micromanagement are two manifestations of the same underlying failure to tolerate uncertainty**.

Neither necessarily addresses the underlying problem: a lack of trust, shared understanding, and the ability of people closest to the work to exercise their own judgment.

And both can produce a peculiar sort of organizational paralysis. Employees learn to anticipate what leadership wants rather than independently determine what the business needs. They become very good at providing status updates, satisfying processes, and responding to whatever is urgent today.

Meanwhile, leaders wonder why their employees don't think more strategically.

## The discipline of doing nothing

In [*Leaders Let it Go*](/2020/08/24/leaders-let-it-go/), I quoted [a piece from Stay SaaSy about the responsibility leaders have to avoid creating chaos](https://staysaasy.com/management/2020/07/07/dont-create-chaos.html "The fancier your title, the more you must avoid causing chaos."). An executive's passing concern can become an entire team's emergency. The higher someone rises in an organization, the greater the consequences of their reactions.

This doesn't mean leaders should be passive, indifferent, or unwilling to make difficult decisions. Nor does it mean suppressing emotion. It mean being able to recognize one's own anxiety, frustration, or uncertainty without immediately converting those feelings into instructions for everyone else.

Sometimes the appropriate response to an uncomfortable feeling is to investigate. Sometimes it's to make a decision. And sometimes it's simply to live with the feeling for a while.

In *[Time to think](/2020/10/28/time-to-think/)*, I ended with a thought from Ashley Williams that has stayed with me: _effective leadership can involve spending a great deal of time sitting quietly with uncomfortable feelings._

We talk a lot about decisiveness, accountability, transparency, and alignment. We build tools and frameworks to make organizations more predictable. These things have their place. But perhaps one of the most valuable things a leader can do is develop the capacity to be uncertain without making that uncertainty everyone else's problem.

An organization’s ability to succeed depends, at least in part, on whether its leaders can resist the urge to exercise their own judgment about everything.
